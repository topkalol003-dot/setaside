The IRS underpayment penalty: what it really costs

If you didn't pay enough estimated tax during the year, the IRS adds an underpayment penalty. It sounds scary, but it works like interest, and it's usually much smaller than people expect. Here's how it's calculated and how to keep it at zero.

How the penalty is calculated

The penalty is figured separately for each of the four due dates. For each one, the IRS looks at:

  1. How much you were short on that due date, compared with the required installment (generally a quarter of the safe harbor amount).
  2. How long it stayed unpaid: from the due date until you paid it, or until the April filing deadline.
  3. The IRS underpayment interest rate for that period: the federal short-term rate plus 3 percentage points, reset every quarter. It was 7% a year for the second half of 2026 (check the IRS "Quarterly interest rates" page for the current rate).

Roughly: amount short × rate × days late ÷ 365.

The takeaway: missing one payment won't ruin you, but skipping all four on a $12,000 tax bill can cost several hundred dollars for no benefit. And the penalty is separate from the failure-to-pay penalty and interest that start if you don't pay the full balance by the April deadline.

When you generally won't owe it

  • You owe less than $1,000 after withholding and credits.
  • You paid at least 90% of this year's tax or 100% of last year's (110% if last year's AGI was over $150,000), on time, through estimated payments plus withholding. See the safe harbor guide.
  • You had no tax liability last year and were a US citizen or resident for the whole year.

How to reduce it if you're already behind

  • Pay now. The penalty grows by the day, so a late payment still cuts it. Use IRS Direct Pay ("Estimated tax", Form 1040-ES).
  • Raise W-2 withholding if you also have a job. Withholding is treated as paid evenly through the year, which can cover earlier quarters. More in side hustle taxes with a W-2 job.
  • Uneven income? The annualized income installment method (Form 2210, Schedule AI) can lower the penalty if most of your income came late in the year.
  • Waivers exist for casualties, disasters and some unusual circumstances, and for some people who retired or became disabled. See the Form 2210 instructions.
