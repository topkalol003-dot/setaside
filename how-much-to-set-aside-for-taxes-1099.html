How much should you set aside for taxes as a 1099 contractor?

The usual advice is "put away 25 to 30%". That is a reasonable start, but the right number depends on how much you make, whether you also have a W-2 job, and your state. Here is how to find yours.

The short answer for 2027

For federal tax only (self-employment tax plus income tax), with no other income and the standard deduction:

Calculated with the SetAside Sheet calculator using IRS 2027 brackets, the 2027 standard deduction and the 2027 Social Security wage base. "Married filing jointly" assumes the spouse has no income. State income tax is extra.

Why the percentage changes

Self-employment tax is a flat 15.3% on 92.35% of profit, about 14.1% of every dollar, from the first dollar once you reach $400. That is why even low earners should save around 14%.

Income tax is progressive. The standard deduction ($16,600 single, $33,200 joint in 2027) is taxed at 0%, and each bracket above it is taxed at a higher rate. The more you earn, the bigger the share that goes to tax.

Three things that push your number up

  1. A W-2 job on top. Your salary already fills the lower brackets, so freelance income lands in higher ones (full guide). Example: $60,000 salary with $5,500 withheld, plus $30,000 of freelance profit (single, 2027). You need to set aside about 29.9% of that freelance profit, not 18%.
  2. State income tax. Anywhere from 0% (for example Texas or Florida) to over 9% at higher incomes in some states.
  3. Other income like interest, a partner's salary or investment gains.

…and things that bring it down

  • Business expenses. Tax is on profit, not revenue. Track every deductible cost.
  • Retirement contributions to a SEP-IRA or Solo 401(k) reduce income tax.
  • The QBI deduction lets many freelancers deduct up to 20% of qualified business income. Our calculator leaves it out on purpose, so its estimate is on the safe side.

A simple system that works

  1. Open a separate savings account just for taxes.
  2. Every time a client pays you, move your percentage into it the same day.
  3. Pay the IRS from that account on each due date.
  4. Every month or two, update your numbers. If income is running higher than you planned, raise the percentage.
